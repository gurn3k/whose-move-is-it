A Kubernetes pull request waits 3 days for review, then merges in under 2 hours

By Gurnek Khaira · Snapshot of 21 November 2026 · 1,526 merged and 1,367 open pull requests in kubernetes/kubernetes

For 1 in 10, the review wait reaches about 6 weeks.

Software teams make changes through “pull requests” (PRs), proposals that a colleague must review and approve before they go in. This report looks at Kubernetes, one of the world’s largest open-source projects, and finds that changes spend almost all their time waiting to be reviewed and approved; once approved, they merge within hours. It shows which teams’ review queues hold the most waiting, and every number links to the real changes behind it, so anyone can check it.

Key findings

  1. 93% of the waiting happens before anyone approves a change. Getting a reviewer’s lgtm (“looks good to me”) took a median , and about 42 days or longer for the slowest 1 in 10. Once both review labels were set, merging took a median 1.7 hours. See Figure 1
  2. 47% of the open PRs are waiting on a reviewer. 641 of 1,367 open PRs (47%) wait on a reviewer or approver, and open PRs, 22% of the backlog, have had no human response, for a median of 37 days. See Figure 2
  3. Two review queues hold 23% of all the waiting. of the PRs waiting for review (182 of 303) belong to sig/api-machinery and sig/node. Kubernetes calls its teams SIGs, short for special interest groups. See Figure 3
  4. Two of 21 teams are on 61% of the open PRs. Of the 1,042 open PRs that aren’t on hold, are labeled for sig/api-machinery or sig/node. See Figure 4

2. 47% of the open PRs are waiting on a reviewer

Every open PR is waiting on someone: a reviewer, an approver, its own author, or no one because it’s parked. Each square below is one PR. Click a group to see its PRs.

The author’s move is almost as common as the reviewer’s

383 open PRs are waiting on their author, against 303 waiting for a reviewer’s lgtm. Of the author’s queue, 230 need a rebase and only 19 have requested changes; the other 134 are waiting on a process label or the contributor licence check, most often a release note.

Approvals are not the constraint

Only 43 PRs have lgtm and are waiting for an approver, and 18 are waiting to merge.

3. Two review queues hold 23% of all the waiting

Each open PR sits in one team’s queue for one stage. Queues are ranked by PR-days of waiting, which counts both how many PRs wait and how long: 10 PRs waiting 10 days is 100 PR-days, the same as one PR waiting 100 days. The unit is a team and a stage, never a person, because a long queue reflects capacity and process rather than anyone’s effort.

Method and limits

How it was measured

  • Timelines come straight from GitHub’s API for every PR merged in 90 days and every open PR.
  • Review and approval are the lgtm and approved labels Kubernetes’ merge bot sets. If a label is removed and added again, the last add counts, so rework shows up as review time.
  • Bots and the PR’s own author never count as a response.
  • Only medians and p90 are shown. Groups under 10 PRs show — and are never ranked.
  • Figure 1 is the one exception: it splits each merged PR’s wait into consecutive stages and adds the days up across PRs, so long waits count for more.

What it can’t show

  • Labels are a proxy: a PR waiting on a reviewer who is away looks the same as one waiting because the change is hard.
  • This is one snapshot, not a trend, and it doesn’t measure reviewer capacity.
  • It names teams and stages, never people.

Checked against DevStats

Kubernetes’ own DevStats charts PR velocity over time. Recomputing its “time to approve and merge” definitions on this data, for PRs created in August and September 2026, gives the same picture: most of the time goes to waiting for lgtm, and merging after approval takes hours. The medians differ by 16 to 28%; the cross-check explains the likely causes.
